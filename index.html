What you're looking at

A lab bench with two cylinders of the same gas and a glass ‘energy landscape’. The left cylinder measures something that's really hard to measure. The right one measures something easy. Watch the two glowing bars.

Why it matters: entropy, a gas's hidden disorder, can't be read off a dial. In the 1870s James Clerk Maxwell showed that because a gas's energy is a smooth landscape, how much disorder it gains when it expands is always exactly equal to how fast its pressure rises when you warm it in a sealed box. Engineers use this swap every day to work out things no instrument can see.

How to use it

  1. Press Run both experiments. Left: the gas expands while a warm bath feeds it heat. Right: the piston is locked and the gas is warmed.
  2. Compare the two bars. They match, every time.
  3. Change the temperature or drag the piston, then run again. Different numbers, still a match.
  4. Open Advanced to try a real gas, the other three relations, or Show why on the glass landscape.
  5. Reset to example puts everything back.
